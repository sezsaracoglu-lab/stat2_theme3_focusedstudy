📜 The rules of the game

Ten rules that govern the whole method. Each step then has two or three of its own, behind the 📜 chip inside that step.

  1. Chi-square is for counts of people in categories. Every variable involved is qualitative — two for independence, one for goodness of fit — and each individual is counted in exactly one cell. Percentages, means and scores never go into the cells.
  2. Count the variables, not the categories. Two variables means a grid and the test of INDEPENDENCE. One variable means a row and GOODNESS OF FIT. A 2 × 7 table is still two variables.
  3. The margins describe; only the conditional distributions show a relationship. A marginal percentage divides by N. A conditional percentage divides by that group’s own total — and a relationship IS a difference between conditional distributions.
  4. H₀ gets written twice. “There is no relation between the two variables”, and “the percentages are the same in the different groups”. The second wording is the one you can build a table from.
  5. Expected counts come from the margins alone. E = row total × column total ÷ grand total. Only margins and N may appear; an individual cell count entering on its own is an error. Never round them.
  6. One formula, always. χ² = Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E, over every cell. Squared so the deviations cannot cancel, divided by E so each gap is scaled to the size of its cell.
  7. df comes from the SHAPE of the table, never from N. (r − 1)(c − 1) for independence; k − 1 for goodness of fit. A 2 × 2 table has df = 1 whether you tested 100 people or 100 000.
  8. More cells means a higher bar. Every cell adds a positive amount even under H₀, so a bigger table collects a bigger χ² by accident. The critical values rise with df to compensate.
  9. Check the expected counts before you report the p-value. Average expected count at least 5, every individual EXPECTED count at least 1 — and in a 2 × 2 table, all four expected counts at least 5.
  10. A significant χ² says “related”, and stops there. It cannot say which way the arrow points, and it cannot rule out a third variable driving both. Random assignment is what would rule that out.

🎯 Learning objectives

  • Decide from a scenario whether it needs a chi-square test of independence, a goodness-of-fit test, or neither — and say why.
  • Read a two-way table: name the two variables, identify the joint, marginal and grand totals, and check that the margins reconcile.
  • Compute a marginal distribution in percentages and say what it cannot tell you.
  • Compute conditional distributions, choose the right denominator, and graph them side by side or stacked.
  • State H₀ and H₁ in both the lecture’s wordings, and explain why a chi-square alternative is never directional.
  • Explain why two tables with identical margins can show completely different relationships.
  • Reproduce the attendance example from memory: 35-5-40-20, its margins and its two conditional percentages.
  • Compute every expected count from the margins, keep them unrounded, and check them against the margins.
  • Compute χ² = Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E and explain what the squaring and the division each contribute.
  • Find df for any table, read the χ² table at the right row, and turn the comparison into a p-value range and a decision.
  • Write the conclusion in the words of the study, then work through all three causal explanations and say which the design rules out.
  • Apply the same four steps to a larger table, check the three validity conditions, and run a goodness-of-fit test with df = k − 1.

🏛️ The whole method in one paragraph

Start by counting the variables: two qualitative variables crossed in a grid means the test of independence, one row of counts against a claim means goodness of fit. Read the table — name the two variables, check that both sets of margins sum to the same N — and describe each variable on its own with a marginal distribution, knowing it cannot show a relationship. Then condition: work out the percentages within each group, compare them, and graph the comparison, because a relationship IS a difference between conditional distributions. State H₀ twice — no relation, and the same percentages in every group — and notice that the second wording names a specific table, because given the margins there is exactly one table with identical percentages. Build it: expected count = row total × column total ÷ grand total, from the margins alone and never rounded. Measure the gap with χ² = Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E, squared so deviations cannot cancel and divided by E so each gap is scaled to its cell. Judge it against the critical value at df = (r − 1)(c − 1), remembering that more cells means a higher bar and that N never enters df. Check the expected counts before you trust the p-value. Then write the conclusion in the words of the study — “related”, with the direction of the percentages — and stop: a significant χ² cannot say which way the arrow points, and cannot rule out a third variable producing the whole table on its own. For goodness of fit, everything is identical except that E comes from a claimed proportion times n, and df is k − 1.

🗺️ Which chi-square does this need?

Where the test sits, and the one question that picks the version.

⬇ builds onthe course flowchart — data type first, purpose second⬆ hands upa named test: independence, or goodness of fit
  • ▸Chi-square is for FREQUENCIES. Both variables qualitative, counts of people in categories, nothing to average.
  • ▸Two variables → the test of INDEPENDENCE. It tells you about the relation between two nominal variables.
  • ▸One variable → GOODNESS OF FIT. It tells you how well the observed frequencies match a prior assumption.
🪝 memory hookCount the variables, not the categories. Two variables means a grid, and a grid asks “is there a relation?”. One variable means a row, and a row asks “does it match what I claimed?”.

In words

Chi-square is the test for frequencies: both variables qualitative, counts of people in categories, nothing to average. There are two versions and one question picks between them — how many variables am I counting people on? Two means a grid, and the grid asks whether the two are related. One means a row, and the row asks whether it matches a claim.

  • On the course flowchart, chi-square is the nominal-data column: goodness of fit on the single-sample row, independence on the two-independent-groups row.
  • The commonest error is upstream of the test. If either variable is really quantitative, the answer is not a better chi-square — it is a different test.

Rules of the game for step 1

  • Count the VARIABLES, not the categories. A 2 × 7 table is still two variables, and a six-colour bag of sweets is still one.
  • Every variable involved must be qualitative — two for independence, one for goodness of fit — and the numbers in the cells must be COUNTS of individuals, never percentages, means or scores.
  • Each individual is counted in exactly one cell. If someone can appear twice, this is not the right test.

Say it to a friend

“Chi-square is what you use when all you have are counts in categories. If I’ve got two categorical variables and want to know whether they’re related, that’s the test of independence. If I’ve got one and want to check it against a claim, that’s goodness of fit.”

Key insight

Both tests do exactly the same arithmetic — Σ (observed − expected)² ⁄ expected. They differ only in where the expected counts come from: from the table’s own margins for independence, from an external claim for goodness of fit.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Running a chi-square on a quantitative variable that someone has chopped into categories. It will produce a number, but you have thrown away most of your information, and the right answer was a different test altogether.

🧪 Demo 1 · Which test does this need?

Eight short scenarios. Independence, goodness of fit, or no chi-square at all — decide, then check.

Check yourself

A researcher records each of 200 people’s blood type and whether they donate. Which test, and why?
The test of independence. Two qualitative variables — blood type and donor status — measured on the same people, with each person counted in exactly one cell of the grid. The question is whether the two are related.
A bag of 60 sweets is sorted by colour and compared with the manufacturer’s advertised percentages. Which test, and where do the expected counts come from?
Goodness of fit: one variable, one row of counts. The expected counts come from the CLAIM — each advertised proportion multiplied by 60 — not from the data. That is the only difference between the two tests.

🧱 Read the table

Body, margins, grand total — three distributions in one table, answering three questions.

⬇ builds ona decision that this is a two-variable, count-based question⬆ hands upthe row totals, the column totals and N — everything the test will need
  • ▸The BODY holds the joint distribution — how many people fall into each combination of the two variables.
  • ▸The MARGINS hold each variable alone. Row totals describe the row variable; column totals describe the column variable.
  • ▸Everything must reconcile: row totals and column totals both sum to the grand total. That is the first check on any table you are given.
🪝 memory hookA cross-table is a block of flats. The flats are the joint counts, the totals down the side and along the bottom are how many people live on each floor and in each stairwell, and the corner number is the whole building.

In words

A two-way table counts people by two things at once. The body holds the joint distribution: how many people fall into each combination. The margins hold each variable on its own. The corner is everybody. Three different distributions live in one table and they answer three different questions, so the first skill is saying which part you are looking at.

  • Size matters later. “Rows × columns” is how you describe the table, and it is what fixes the degrees of freedom.
  • Two exam questions, always: what are the variables described by this table, and how many individuals were surveyed?

Rules of the game for step 2

  • Check that the margins add up before you do anything else. A table that does not reconcile has a transcription error in it.
  • Name the two variables out loud. “Rows are school type, columns are how much they studied” is the sentence that stops every later confusion.
  • N is the grand total, not the number of cells and not a row total. Every expected count is divided by it.

Say it to a friend

“A two-way table counts people by two things at once. The inside tells you about the combinations, the edges tell you about each variable on its own, and the corner is everybody.”

Key insight

The margins and the body answer different questions, and the whole test is built on that split: the margins say what H₀ would predict, and the body says what actually happened. χ² is the distance between the two.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading a cell count as if it were a rate. “35 attenders passed” is not a pass rate — it becomes one only when you divide by the 40 people in that row, which is step 4’s job.

Check yourself

A table has row totals 100 and 150, and column totals 50, 75 and 125. What size is it, and how many people were surveyed?
It is a 2 × 3 table — two rows, three columns. N = 250, and the check is that both sets of margins give it: 100 + 150 = 250 and 50 + 75 + 125 = 250. The size will fix df at (2 − 1)(3 − 1) = 2.
Why does the lecture ask “what are the variables described by this table?” in almost every exam question?
Because naming them forces you to see that the table is about two qualitative variables measured on the same individuals — which is what makes chi-square the right test at all. It also fixes which totals are “rows” and which are “columns” for every formula that follows.

📊 One variable at a time

The edges of the table, turned into percentages — and the thing they cannot tell you.

⬇ builds onthe margins from step 2⬆ hands upa description of each variable on its own, and a reason to go further
  • ▸The marginal distribution of one variable is its distribution among ALL the individuals described by the table.
  • ▸Step 1: use the row or column totals to calculate the distribution in percents.
  • ▸Step 2: make a graph to display it.
🪝 memory hookA marginal distribution is a headcount at the door. You learn how many arrived in each category — and nothing whatsoever about who came with whom.

In words

A marginal distribution describes one of the two variables among ALL the individuals in the table. Use the row or column totals, turn them into percentages, draw them. It is useful background and it is often the first thing an exam asks for — but it is incapable of showing a relationship, because it has already thrown the other variable away.

  • Percentages beat counts, especially when the groups being compared are different sizes.
  • And the limitation: marginal distributions tell us NOTHING about the relationship between the two variables.

Rules of the game for step 3

  • A marginal distribution always divides by the GRAND total. The moment you divide by a row or column total instead, you have moved to step 4.
  • Report percentages, not raw counts, whenever the groups differ in size — otherwise the bigger group simply looks more important.
  • Two marginal distributions can be identical in two tables that tell completely opposite stories. They are background, never evidence.

Say it to a friend

“If I only look at the totals down the side of the table, I’m describing one variable on its own and pretending the other doesn’t exist. It’s useful background, but it can’t show a relationship.”

Key insight

The margins are exactly what the null hypothesis is allowed to know. That is why step 7 builds the expected counts from them and nothing else — they carry every fact about the two variables EXCEPT how they go together.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Answering “is there a relationship?” from the margins. They cannot answer it. Two tables with identical margins can show a strong relationship and none at all — the figure in step 5 is exactly that pair.

Check yourself

In a 2 × 3 table with row totals 100 and 150, what is the marginal distribution of the row variable?
100 ⁄ 250 = 40% and 150 ⁄ 250 = 60%. Both divide by the GRAND total of 250, because a marginal distribution describes that variable among everybody in the table.
You are given only the margins of a table. What can you NOT work out?
Anything about the relationship. The margins fix what each variable looks like on its own, but many different bodies fit the same margins — including one with no relationship at all and one with a strong one. The inside of the table is where the relationship lives.

🎚️ Where the relationship actually lives

Condition on one variable, compare the percentages, and draw the comparison.

⬇ builds ona table whose margins you have read⬆ hands upthe percentages you will actually compare — and a first impression of the result
  • ▸A conditional distribution describes a variable among individuals who have a specific value of ANOTHER variable.
  • ▸Step 1: select the row(s) or column(s) of interest. Step 2: turn them into percents — the denominator is now that group’s own total.
  • ▸Step 3: graph it, with a side-by-side bar graph or a segmented bar graph.
🪝 memory hookConditioning is standing inside one group and looking around. “Among males…” — everything you count from that spot is divided by the number of males, not by everybody.

In words

A conditional distribution describes one variable among the individuals who have a specific value of the other. You stand inside one group and look around: among the attenders, what percentage passed? The denominator is now that group’s own total. Comparing those percentages across groups is the only display that can show a relationship, because a relationship IS a difference between conditional distributions.

  • This is the only display that can show a relationship, because a relationship is a difference between conditional distributions.
  • Conditioning on rows and conditioning on columns give different percentages. Say which one you did.

Rules of the game for step 4

  • The denominator changes. A marginal percentage divides by N; a conditional percentage divides by that row’s or column’s own total.
  • Say “among …” out loud: “among the attenders, 87.5% passed.” If your sentence does not start with “among”, you have probably computed a marginal percentage by accident.
  • Equal conditional distributions = no relationship in this sample. Different ones = a relationship in this sample, and a reason to run the test.

Say it to a friend

“To see whether two categorical variables are related, I work out the percentages separately within each group and compare them. If the percentages are basically the same, there’s no relationship; if they differ, there might be one.”

Key insight

The whole test is a formal version of this picture. χ² asks exactly one question: are the conditional distributions far enough apart that chance alone would rarely produce the gap?

The mistake almost everyone makes

Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes. 35 attenders passed and 40 non-attenders passed — but 35 out of 40 is 87.5% and 40 out of 60 is 66.7%, and the counts point the opposite way from the truth.

The same comparison, stacked

🧪 Demo 2 · The same table, four ways

The young-adults table. Switch between raw counts, the marginal percentages, and the two conditional directions — and watch which view makes the relationship visible.

Check yourself

Of 40 attenders, 35 passed; of 60 non-attenders, 40 passed. Give the two conditional distributions and say what they suggest.
Among attenders: 35 ⁄ 40 = 87.5% passed, 12.5% failed. Among non-attenders: 40 ⁄ 60 = 66.7% passed, 33.3% failed. The two differ by about 21 percentage points, so there is a relationship in this sample — whether it is significant in the population is what the test decides.
Why is a side-by-side or segmented bar graph the right picture here, and a single bar chart of the totals the wrong one?
Because the relationship is a DIFFERENCE between conditional distributions, so the picture has to show one distribution per group, side by side. A single chart of the totals shows a marginal distribution, which cannot display a relationship at all.

⚖️ The null hypothesis — and what it looks like as a table

The lecture writes H₀ twice. The second wording is the one you can compute with.

⬇ builds onconditional distributions you can compare⬆ hands upa precise claim to test, and the shape of the table that claim predicts
  • ▸H₀: no relation between the two variables. H₁: there is a relation. Both are claims about the POPULATION.
  • ▸In other words: H₀ says the percentages are the same in the different groups; H₁ says they are not.
  • ▸Chi-square hypotheses are never directional. Any departure in any direction makes χ² larger, so there is no “expected direction” to state.
🪝 memory hookH₀ is the boring universe — the one where knowing which group someone is in tells you nothing new about the outcome. The test asks how strange your table would be in that universe. And the margins are the frame; the relationship is the painting. You can hang very different pictures in exactly the same frame.

In words

The lecture always writes the null hypothesis two ways, and both are examinable. First: there is no relation between the two variables. Second, and more useful: the percentages are the same in the different groups. The second wording is what you can build a table from — given the margins, there is exactly one table whose conditional distributions are identical, and those are the expected counts.

  • The margins do not change between the two pictures. 100 VWO and 150 HAVO, 50 ⁄ 75 ⁄ 125 across the columns, 250 in total — in both.
  • So a relation is not about the totals at all. It is entirely about how the counts are distributed inside them.

Rules of the game for step 5

  • Write both wordings in the exam. The “no relation” version earns the mark; the “percentages are the same” version is what lets you build the expected counts.
  • H₀ and H₁ are about the population, never about your sample. Your sample already shows a difference — the question is whether the population does.
  • Never write a one-sided chi-square hypothesis. Squaring removes the signs, so the test cannot see direction even in principle.

Say it to a friend

“The null hypothesis says the two things have nothing to do with each other — which is the same as saying the percentages are identical in every group. So I write out the table that would look like that, and compare it with the one I actually got.”

Key insight

Given the margins, there is exactly ONE table with identical conditional distributions. That uniqueness is what makes the test possible: H₀ does not just describe a vague idea, it names a specific table to compare yours against.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Stating a direction — “H₁: attenders pass more often”. χ² squares every deviation, so it cannot distinguish that from attenders passing less often. The alternative is always just “there is a relation”.

Check yourself

Write both wordings of H₀ for the attendance study.
“In the population, attending the lectures is not related to passing the course exam.” And: “In the population, the percentage that passes is the same among attenders and among non-attenders.” The second one is what you compute from.
Two tables have exactly the same row and column totals. Can one show a strong relationship and the other none?
Yes — that is precisely the point of the pair of tables in this step. The margins are the frame and the relationship is the painting. 20 ⁄ 30 ⁄ 50 in both groups is H₀; 10 ⁄ 25 ⁄ 65 against 27 ⁄ 33 ⁄ 40 is a relationship, with identical margins.

🎓 The worked example: 35-5-40-20

A hundred students, two yes/no questions — the example that carries the next four steps.

⬇ builds ona null hypothesis you can state in both wordings⬆ hands upa concrete 2 × 2 table, and a difference worth testing
  • ▸The design. Dependent variable: pass (yes/no). Independent variable: attended (yes/no). Both qualitative, so chi-square is the test.
  • ▸Raw data become counts: one row per student collapses into a 2 × 2 table — 35, 5, 40, 20.
  • ▸The margins: 40 attended and 60 did not; 75 passed and 25 failed; N = 100.
🪝 memory hook35-5-40-20. Four numbers, one lecture. If you can write the table from memory, you can rebuild every calculation that follows.

In words

One question: is the percentage of students that passed the same among those who attended the lectures and those who did not? Dependent variable pass (yes/no), independent variable attended (yes/no), both qualitative. A hundred rows of raw data collapse into four numbers, and those four numbers carry the next four steps.

  • The conditional distributions: 87.5% of attenders passed, against 66.7% of non-attenders.
  • So there is a relation in this sample. The only remaining question is whether it holds in the population.

Rules of the game for step 6

  • Collapse the raw data into counts before anything else. Chi-square never sees the individual rows.
  • Read the conditional distributions before you compute. If they are nearly identical, a significant χ² would be a surprise worth double-checking.
  • A difference in the SAMPLE is never the finding. It is the thing you are about to test.

Say it to a friend

“Out of 100 students, 40 went to the lectures and 35 of those passed — 87.5%. Of the 60 who didn’t go, 40 passed — about 67%. So attenders did better in this sample. Now I need to know whether a gap that size could just be luck.”

Key insight

Everything from here on is mechanical. The margins (40, 60, 75, 25, 100) generate the expected counts; the expected counts and the observed counts generate χ²; the table size generates df. Nothing new enters.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Writing the table transposed and then mixing up which total is a “row” total. Fix the orientation once — here, attendance down the side and pass/fail across the top — and keep it for every formula.

Check yourself

From 35, 5, 40, 20, write out all five margins.
Row totals: 35 + 5 = 40 attended, 40 + 20 = 60 did not. Column totals: 35 + 40 = 75 passed, 5 + 20 = 25 failed. Grand total N = 100, and it checks both ways: 40 + 60 = 100 and 75 + 25 = 100.
Why does the lecture report 87.5% and 66.7% rather than 35 and 40?
Because the two groups are different sizes — 40 attenders against 60 non-attenders. The raw counts suggest non-attenders did better (40 passed against 35), while the rates show the opposite. Only the conditional percentages are comparable.

🧮 The table H₀ predicts

One formula — and the reason it is not arbitrary.

⬇ builds onthe margins of the observed table⬆ hands upan expected count for every cell, and the validity check
  • ▸The formula: expected count = row total × column total ÷ grand total.
  • ▸Why it works: if H₀ is true the pass rate is 75% for everyone, so 75% of the 40 attenders — 30 — should pass.
  • ▸Expected counts come ONLY from the margins, never from the inside of the table. That is what makes them a prediction rather than a description.
🪝 memory hookRow times column over total. Or in words: “your row’s share of the people, given the column’s overall rate.”

In words

Expected count = row total × column total ÷ grand total. It looks like a trick until you say it in words: if H₀ is true, the pass rate is 75% for everyone, so 75% of the 40 attenders — 30 — should pass. The formula is the null hypothesis written out as counts. And because it uses only the margins, it knows nothing about how your table actually turned out.

  • They need not be whole numbers. 34.22 bottles of wine is a perfectly good expected count — it is an average, not a person.
  • They double as the validity check: the slide flags expected count ≥ 5 right here.

Rules of the game for step 7

  • Only row totals, column totals and N may appear. If an individual cell count enters on its own — rather than inside a margin — you have made an error.
  • Never round expected counts to whole numbers. Rounding here changes χ², sometimes enough to change the decision.
  • The expected counts must add to the same margins as the observed ones. That is your check: 30 + 10 = 40 ✓, 30 + 45 = 75 ✓.

Say it to a friend

“If the two variables had nothing to do with each other, each group would split the same way as everybody overall. So I take the overall rate and apply it to each group’s size — that’s the expected count.”

Key insight

The expected table always has exactly the same margins as the observed one. That is not a coincidence: it is what “use only the margins” means, and it is the fastest check you have on four or nine pieces of arithmetic.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Using the grand total as the denominator but a cell count as a numerator. The numerator is always a row total times a column total — two margin numbers, never a number from inside the table.

Check yourself

For the attendance table, compute all four expected counts and check them.
Attend-pass: 40 × 75 ⁄ 100 = 30. Attend-fail: 40 × 25 ⁄ 100 = 10. Not-attend-pass: 60 × 75 ⁄ 100 = 45. Not-attend-fail: 60 × 25 ⁄ 100 = 15. The check: 30 + 10 = 40 ✓, 45 + 15 = 60 ✓, 30 + 45 = 75 ✓, 10 + 15 = 25 ✓.
An expected count comes out as 34.22. Should you round it to 34?
No. An expected count is an average over many hypothetical samples, not a number of people, so a fraction is meaningful and correct. Rounding changes every (O − E)² ⁄ E term it appears in, and with small cells it can move χ² enough to change the decision.

➕ Adding up the surprise

Four cells, four small numbers, one total — and what the total means.

⬇ builds onan observed count and an expected count for every cell⬆ hands upa single number: how far this table sits from the one H₀ predicted
  • ▸The statistic: χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² ÷ expected, over all cells.
  • ▸Squaring removes the signs, so over- and under-counts cannot cancel — and big gaps count far more than small ones.
  • ▸Dividing by the expected count scales each gap to the size of the cell. A gap of 5 is trivial where 500 were expected and dramatic where 10 were.
🪝 memory hookEach cell pays a fine for being different from what H₀ predicted — squared, and scaled by how big the cell should have been. χ² is the total fine.

In words

χ² = the sum, across every cell, of (observed − expected)² ÷ expected. Each cell pays a fine for differing from the prediction: squared, so over- and under-counts cannot cancel and big gaps count far more than small ones, and divided by the expected count, so the fine is scaled to how big that cell should have been. χ² is the total fine.

  • χ² = 0 means observed equals expected in every cell. Small deviations are still likely even when H₀ is true.
  • The logic: big difference between observed and expected → large χ² → small p-value → reject H₀.

Rules of the game for step 8

  • Divide by the EXPECTED count, never by the observed one. The expected count is what sets the scale the deviation is being judged against.
  • Every cell contributes, including the ones that match well. Leaving out a cell because its term is small is still an error.
  • χ² can never be negative. If yours is, you have subtracted after squaring, or divided by the wrong thing.

Say it to a friend

“For every cell I take how far off it was, square it, and divide by what was expected there. Adding those up gives χ². Zero means the table matched the null perfectly; the bigger it gets, the worse the null looks.”

Key insight

In a 2 × 2 table all four deviations are the same size — if one cell is 5 above its expected count, the others must be 5 off too, because the margins are fixed. The four terms differ only because they are divided by different expected counts.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Forgetting the square, or forgetting the division. (O − E) alone sums to zero in every table; (O − E)² alone ignores how big the cell was supposed to be. Both parts are doing real work.

Two cells are each 5 away from expectation. One expected 10, the other expected 500. Which contributes more to χ², and by how much?
The small one, by a factor of fifty. 25 ⁄ 10 = 2.50 against 25 ⁄ 500 = 0.05. Being five people off is a big deal when you expected ten and almost nothing when you expected five hundred — which is exactly what the division by E is for.

📐 How big is too big?

It depends on the size of the table — so the bar moves, and df tells you where.

⬇ builds ona χ² value, and the size of the table it came from⬆ hands upa critical value, and therefore a decision
  • ▸df = (number of rows − 1) × (number of columns − 1). For a 2 × 2 table, df = 1.
  • ▸The larger χ² is, the smaller the probability under H₀ — that is the whole reading of the table.
  • ▸More cells means a bigger χ² by accident, so with more df, χ² must be relatively larger before it counts as significant.
🪝 memory hookdf is a handicap. The more cells your table has, the more chances it had to look odd by luck, so the bar is raised before you are allowed to claim a finding.

In words

A χ² of 5.56 means nothing on its own, because bigger tables produce bigger χ² values by accident — more cells, more chances to look odd by luck. Degrees of freedom is the handicap: df = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1). Look up your df, read across to your α, and compare.

  • Read the row for your df and the column for your α, then compare: χ² above the critical value → reject H₀.
  • df has nothing to do with N. A 2 × 2 table has df = 1 whether you tested 100 people or 100 000.

Rules of the game for step 9

  • df = (r − 1)(c − 1) — a PRODUCT, not a sum, and built from the number of rows and columns, never from N.
  • Chi-square uses only the right-hand tail. A large χ² is evidence against H₀; a χ² near zero is not evidence against anything.
  • Compare against the critical value for YOUR df. The same χ² can be significant on 1 df and nowhere near it on 6.

Say it to a friend

“How big χ² has to be depends on the size of the table. I work out (rows − 1) times (columns − 1), look that row up in the chi-square table, and compare my value with the number in the 0.05 column.”

Key insight

Sample size and table size do opposite jobs. A bigger N makes a real difference easier to detect; a bigger table raises the bar. df tracks only the second, which is why it never mentions N.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Computing df as N − 1, or as the number of cells. For a 2 × 2 table tested on a thousand people, df is 1 — not 999 and not 4.

✅ Decide — and then be careful

How to write the conclusion, and the three explanations that survive a significant test.

⬇ builds ona χ², a df and a critical value⬆ hands upa sentence about the population — and an honest list of what it does not establish
  • ▸The decision: p = .018, so H₀ is rejected — attending the lectures is related to the chance of passing the course exam.
  • ▸Or in percentage language: the percentage that passed is larger in the group that attended than in the group that did not.
  • ▸Primary interpretation: attending prepares you, so your chance of passing rises.
🪝 memory hookχ² is a smoke detector. It tells you reliably that something is burning; it has no opinion whatsoever about what lit the fire.

In words

p = .018, so H₀ is rejected: attending the lectures is related to the chance of passing. Then comes the part that carries the marks. A significant χ² says the two variables are related. It says nothing about which causes which, and nothing about whether a third variable is behind both.

  • Reversed relation? Here implausible — the exam takes place after the lectures, so passing cannot have caused attendance.
  • Third variable? Motivation would produce this table on its own: motivated students attend, and motivated students pass.

Rules of the game for step 10

  • Write the conclusion about the POPULATION and in the words of the study. “Attending is related to passing” — not “χ² was significant”.
  • Then work through all three explanations out loud: x → y, y → x, and a third variable driving both. Dismissing one needs a reason, usually about timing or design.
  • Nobody was randomly assigned to attend. Without random assignment, “related” is the strongest claim the test can support.

Say it to a friend

“The p-value was .018, so the difference is bigger than chance would usually give — attendance and passing really are related. But students chose whether to attend, so motivation could be behind both, and the test can’t separate those.”

Key insight

The reversed-relation explanation is ruled out here by TIMING, not by statistics — the exam came after the lectures. That is a fact about the study’s design, not about its numbers, and it is the only one of the three explanations you can dismiss without collecting different data.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Writing “attending the lectures increases your chance of passing” as the conclusion. That is a causal claim from an observational study, and the third explanation — motivation — produces the same table without it.

🧪 Demo 7 · Name the alternative explanation

Six significant chi-square results. For each, decide which alternative explanation is the serious one — reversed direction, a third variable, or neither (a design that actually supports the causal reading).

Check yourself

Write the conclusion for the attendance study in one sentence, correctly.
“There is a significant relation between attending the lectures and passing the exam, χ²(1) = 5.56, p = .018: in the population, the percentage that passes is higher among attenders than among non-attenders.” It names the population, reports the statistic with its df, and stops short of causation.
Why can the reversed explanation be dismissed here but the third-variable one cannot?
Because of timing: the exam takes place after the lectures, so passing cannot have caused attendance. There is no equivalent argument against motivation — motivated students both attend and pass, which would produce exactly this table with no causal link between the two at all. Only random assignment would rule that out.

🍷 Bigger tables, and when the test is allowed

The same four steps on a 3 × 3 — and the three conditions that decide whether the p-value means anything.

⬇ builds onthe whole four-step method from steps 6 to 10⬆ hands upa method that scales to any table size, with its validity conditions attached
  • ▸The study: 243 bottles, classified by the wine’s origin and by the music — none, French accordion, or Italian string.
  • ▸Conditional distributions first. With no music French wine was 30 ⁄ 84 = 35.7%; with French music, 39 ⁄ 75 = 52.0%.
  • ▸Expected counts from the same formula: French wine with no music, 99 × 84 ÷ 243 = 34.22.
🪝 memory hookThe lonely Italian bottle. One bottle where ten were expected is worth more than half a dozen cells being slightly off — small expected counts make big contributions.

In words

Nothing new, just bigger. 243 bottles of wine classified by origin and by the music playing: conditional distributions first, then expected counts from the same formula, then nine (O − E)² ⁄ E terms, then df = (3 − 1)(3 − 1) = 4. What IS new is the validity check, because bigger tables spread the same people over more cells and the approximation starts to fail.

  • χ² is the sum of nine terms: 0.52 + 2.33 + … + 0.42 = 18.28, with df = (3 − 1)(3 − 1) = 4, and p between .001 and .0025.
  • Three conditions: the average expected count at least 5; every individual EXPECTED count at least 1; and in a 2 × 2 table, all four expected counts at least 5.

Rules of the game for step 11

  • Check the expected counts BEFORE reporting the p-value. If the conditions fail, the number is not a p-value, it is a decoration.
  • Big percentages in small groups prove nothing. Tutor 7’s 55% comes from 11 students out of 20, and the table is not significant.
  • Two sampling designs are valid: independent samples from two or more populations, each individual classified on one variable; or a single sample with each individual classified on both.

Say it to a friend

“Same four steps, bigger table. Work out the percentages within each music condition, build the expected counts from the margins, add up the nine (O − E)² ⁄ E terms, and look up 18.28 with 4 degrees of freedom. It clears the .0025 column (16.42) but falls just short of the .001 one (18.47), so p is between .001 and .0025 — the music matters.”

Key insight

A big table is not a harder test, but it asks more of your data. The same number of people spread over more cells means smaller expected counts, bigger random fluctuation and a higher bar — which is why both the df rule and the validity conditions exist.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading a dramatic-looking percentage from a tiny group as a finding. The validity conditions and the df handicap exist precisely because small cells are noisy, and a non-significant table does not become significant because one row looks striking.

Big tables, and when the test is allowed

Check yourself

df = (3 − 1)(3 − 1) = 4. The critical value at α = .05 is 9.49 and at α = .001 it is about 18.47, so 18.28 sits just below the .001 column — p falls between .001 and .0025. Comfortably significant.
Your 2 × 2 table has expected counts 4.1, 7.9, 15.9 and 30.1. May you run the test?
No. In a 2 × 2 table ALL FOUR expected counts must be at least 5, and 4.1 fails. The average expected count rule is not enough here — the 2 × 2 condition is stricter, exactly because the approximation is worst in the smallest tables.

🍫 Goodness of fit: one variable against a claim

The second flavour — identical arithmetic, expected counts from a promise.

⬇ builds onthe χ² statistic and the idea of an expected count⬆ hands upa verdict on whether observed frequencies match a stated claim
  • ▸One variable, one row of counts. A one-way table displays the distribution of a categorical variable for the individuals in a sample.
  • ▸H₀ is a LIST of proportions — .24 blue, .20 orange, .16 green, .14 yellow, .13 red, .13 brown. H₁: at least one proportion differs from the claim.
  • ▸Expected count = claimed proportion × sample size. Blue: 0.24 × 60 = 14.40.
🪝 memory hookIndependence builds its expectations from the table; goodness of fit builds them from a promise someone made. Everything after that is the same arithmetic.

In words

The second flavour of chi-square, and the only thing that changes is where the expected counts come from. One variable, one row of counts, and a claim about the proportions. Multiply each claimed proportion by the sample size to get what you would expect, then run exactly the same Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E — with df = k − 1, because there is only one variable.

  • Same statistic: χ² = 2.025 + 1.333 + 0.600 + 5.186 + 0.621 + 0.415 = 10.180.
  • df = k − 1 = 5, giving p between .05 and .10 — so we fail to reject H₀ and do not conclude the company’s claim is wrong.

Rules of the game for step 12

  • df = k − 1, where k is the number of CATEGORIES. There is no (r − 1)(c − 1) here, because there is only one variable.
  • The claimed proportions must sum to 1, and the expected counts must then sum to n. Both are free checks.
  • “Fail to reject” is not “the claim is true”. It means the data are compatible with the claim — with 60 sweets, quite a lot of claims would be.

Say it to a friend

“Someone claims the percentages should be 24, 20, 16, 14, 13, 13. I multiply each by my sample size to get what I’d expect, compare with what I counted, and add up the (O − E)² ⁄ E terms. With six categories that’s 5 degrees of freedom.”

Key insight

The two tests are one test. Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E never changes; only the source of E and the formula for df do. If you can say where E came from, you can do both.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Using (r − 1)(c − 1) on a one-way table. A row of six counts is not a 1 × 6 table for df purposes — that formula would give 0. It is k − 1 = 5.

H₀ is always the no-relation claim, which in percentage language is “the same percentages in every group”.

✗a. A chi-square alternative is never directional — squaring removes the sign, so the test cannot see direction at all.
✗b. H₀ is a claim about the POPULATION. Your sample’s observed counts essentially never equal the expected ones exactly, even when H₀ is true.
✗d. That is H₁, the alternative.
🏷️ Write both wordings: “no relation”, and “the same percentages in every group”.
Question 12 · step 8 · add up the surprise
If observed counts equal expected counts in every cell, what is χ²?
  • a.Undefined
  • b.0
  • c.1
  • d.Equal to the degrees of freedom
Every term is (O − E)² ÷ E = 0, so the total is 0. In practice this essentially never happens — small deviations are still likely even when H₀ is true.
The other options
✗a. It is perfectly well defined. χ² is undefined only if an expected count were zero, which the validity conditions rule out.
✗c. Nothing in the formula produces a floor of 1; every term is a squared difference.
✗d. Under H₀ the statistic averages about df across repeated samples — a different thing from its value when every cell matches exactly.
🏷️ χ² = 0 is the table H₀ predicted, exactly. It is a floor, not evidence FOR H₀.
Question 13 · step 11 · bigger tables
Before trusting the p-value of a 2 × 2 chi-square test, which condition must hold?
  • a.All four observed cell counts are at least 5
  • b.The sample size is at least 100
  • c.Both of the variables are normally distributed
  • d.All four expected cell counts are at least 5
The 2 × 2 condition is the strict one: all four EXPECTED counts at least 5. For larger tables the rule relaxes to an average expected count of at least 5 with every individual expected count at least 1.
The other options
✗a. The condition is on the expected counts, not the observed ones. An observed count of 2 is fine if its expected count is 8.
✗b. No sample-size rule is stated anywhere. A large N with very lopsided margins can still produce a tiny expected count.
✗c. Normality is irrelevant — these are counts of people in categories, not measurements.
🏷️ Check the expected counts BEFORE you report the p-value, not after.
Question 14 · step 9 · how big is too big
Why must χ² be relatively larger to be significant when a table has many cells?
  • a.Because the formula divides each term by the total number of cells
  • b.Because df is larger, and more cells accumulate a bigger χ² by chance alone
  • c.Because tables with many cells always have small expected counts in them
  • d.Because the sample size is necessarily bigger whenever the table is bigger
Every cell contributes a positive amount even under H₀, so a table with many cells collects a larger total by accident. The critical values rise with df to keep the error rate at α.
The other options
✗a. The formula divides each term by that cell’s expected count, not by the number of cells.
✗c. They often do, which is a separate problem — but it is a validity issue, not the reason the critical value rises.
✗d. Table size and sample size are independent. You can cross two six-category variables on thirty people.
🏷️ More cells = more chances to look odd by luck = a higher bar.
Question 15 · step 11 · bigger tables
In the wine study (row total 99 for French wine, column total 84 for no music, table total 243), what is the expected count for French wine with no music?
  • a.34.22
  • b.30.56
  • c.39.06
  • d.33.00
99 × 84 ÷ 243 = 34.22, and a fractional expected count is perfectly correct — it is an average, not a number of bottles.
The other options
✗b. 30.56 is the expected count for French wine under FRENCH music — the right formula with the wrong column total.
✗c. 39.06 is the expected count for “other” wine with no music — the right column, the wrong row.
✗d. 33.00 is 99 ÷ 3, as if the three music conditions had equal totals. They do not: 84, 75 and 84.
🏷️ Never round an expected count. 34.22 bottles is the right answer.
Question 16 · step 11 · bigger tables
The wine table is 3 × 3 and gives χ² = 18.28. What are the degrees of freedom, and roughly what is the p-value?
  • a.df = 4, and p is greater than .05
  • b.df = 8, and p is below .001
  • c.df = 4, and p is between .001 and .0025
  • d.df = 9, and p is about .03
df = (3 − 1)(3 − 1) = 4. The chi-square table gives 16.42 at p = .0025 and 18.47 at p = .001, and 18.28 falls between them.
The other options
✗a. df is right, but 18.28 is far above the α = .05 critical value of 9.49.
✗b. 8 would be (3 − 1) × (5 − 1) or similar — and 18.28 is just BELOW the .001 critical value of 18.47, not past it.
✗d. 9 is the number of CELLS, not the degrees of freedom.
🏷️ Find the two table columns your χ² sits between and report the range.
Question 17 · step 12 · goodness of fit
Mars claims 24% of M&M’s are blue. In a bag of 60, what is the expected number of blue sweets?
  • a.13.00
  • b.14.40
  • c.9.00
  • d.24.00
0.24 × 60 = 14.40 — the claimed proportion times the sample size.
The other options
✗a. 13.00 is the claimed percentage for red and brown, not blue.
✗c. 9 is the number actually OBSERVED in the bag, which is what you compare against 14.40.
✗d. 24.00 is the percentage read as a count, with the sample size never used.
🏷️ Goodness of fit: expected = claimed proportion × n. The claim supplies E, not the data.
Question 18 · step 12 · goodness of fit
The M&M test gives χ² = 10.180 with df = 5; the critical values are 9.24 at p = .10 and 11.07 at p = .05. What is the conclusion at α = .05?
  • a.p < .05, so we reject H₀ and the claim is wrong
  • b.p < .01, so the company’s claimed distribution is clearly wrong
  • c.The test cannot be used, because some of the counts are small
  • d..05 < p < .10, so we fail to reject H₀
10.180 sits between the two critical values, so p is between .05 and .10 — above α. We fail to reject H₀ and do not have sufficient evidence that the claim is wrong. That is not the same as proving it correct.
The other options
✗a. 10.180 is below the .05 critical value of 11.07, so it does not clear the α = .05 bar.
✗b. Nowhere near: 10.180 does not even clear the .05 bar, let alone .01.
✗c. The expected counts run from 7.8 to 14.4, all comfortably above 5, so the conditions are met.
🏷️ “Fail to reject” means the data are COMPATIBLE with the claim, never that the claim is true.
Question 19 · step 10 · decide, then be careful
The attendance result is significant. Which alternative explanation does the lecture take seriously?
  • a.The chi-square test is not valid for 2 × 2 tables
  • b.The sample of 100 students was far too large
  • c.Motivation makes students both attend and pass
  • d.Passing the exam caused students to attend the lectures
A third variable: if you are motivated you go to the lectures, and if you are motivated you pass. The reversed explanation is ruled out by timing, since the exam comes after the lectures.
The other options
✗a. A 2 × 2 is the standard case for this test, and all four expected counts here are at least 10.
✗b. A large sample is an advantage, not a flaw. It is what makes a real difference detectable.
✗d. Ruled out by timing — the exam takes place after the lectures, so it cannot have caused attendance.
🏷️ Run through all three: x → y, y → x, and z → both. Dismissing one needs a reason.
Question 20 · step 9 · how big is too big
You run a chi-square test on a 2 × 2 table with 5 000 participants. What is df?
  • a.1
  • b.4
  • c.4 999
  • d.It depends on the expected counts
df = (2 − 1)(2 − 1) = 1, regardless of sample size. What a large N changes is the POWER of the test, not the degrees of freedom or the critical value.
The other options
✗b. 4 is the number of cells.
✗c. 4 999 is n − 1, which is the df of a completely different family of tests.
✗d. The expected counts decide whether the test is VALID, not what df it has.
🏷️ df comes from the shape of the table. N never appears in it.
Question 21 · step 2 · read the table
A table of counts has row totals 120 and 80, and column totals 45, 70 and 90. What is wrong?
  • a.Chi-square cannot be used on any table whose row totals are unequal
  • b.The margins do not reconcile — the rows total 200 but the columns total 205
  • c.Nothing is wrong here; row totals and column totals do not have to agree
  • d.The table is 2 × 3, so it cannot have only two row totals listed
Both sets of margins must sum to the same grand total: 120 + 80 = 200 against 45 + 70 + 90 = 205. One of the five numbers has been mistyped, and every expected count would inherit the error.
The other options
✗a. Unequal row totals are completely normal: 40 attenders against 60 non-attenders, for instance.
✗c. They must agree — both sets are counting the same individuals, each person once.
✗d. A 2 × 3 table has exactly two row totals and three column totals. That part is fine.
🏷️ Add the margins both ways before anything else. They must give the same N.
Question 22 · step 6 · the worked example
In the attendance study, 35 of the 40 attenders passed and 40 of the 60 non-attenders passed. A student concludes that non-attenders did better, because 40 is more than 35. What has gone wrong?
  • a.The groups are different sizes, so only the percentages are comparable — 87.5% against 66.7%
  • b.Nothing has gone wrong: 40 passes really is more than 35 passes
  • c.The student should have compared the numbers who failed instead of the passes
  • d.The counts should have been divided by the grand total of 100 before comparing
40 attenders and 60 non-attenders are not comparable groups, so raw counts answer a different question from the one being asked. Among attenders 35 ⁄ 40 = 87.5% passed; among non-attenders 40 ⁄ 60 = 66.7%. The rates point the opposite way from the counts.
The other options
✗b. It is more passes, but out of half again as many people. The question asks which group did better, which is a rate.
✗c. Comparing failures has exactly the same problem: 5 against 20, from groups of 40 and 60.
✗d. Dividing by 100 gives joint percentages — 35% and 40% of everybody — which still ignores that the two groups differ in size.
🏷️ Different group sizes mean different denominators. Condition first, compare second.
